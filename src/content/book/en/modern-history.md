## Martial Arts After World War II

After World War II, the Americans largely banned the martial arts in Japan. Ninjutsu survived anyway. A small group of inconspicuous, devoted men passed the teachings of the ninja on in secret.

Many Ryū, however, died out. Their last grandmaster had no descendants to carry the tradition on. So before they died, many masters destroyed the old scrolls and manuscripts.

Even in the quiet decades before, many martial arts had changed. They became spiritualized: _jutsu_ (術, <q>art</q>) became _dō_ (道, <q>way, path, principle</q>).

## Ishitani Takakage Matsutaro

Not every master accepted this change. Some longed for the days when real martial arts were still taught.

One of them was Ishitani Takakage Matsutaro, a _chūnin_ (中忍) of the Iga-Ryū (伊賀流) and the 26th grandmaster of the _happō hiken_ (八法秘剣) of the Kukishinden-Ryū (九鬼神伝流). He despised the way the arts were being reduced to sports or Zen movements, and he opposed the change. In the end he had neither students nor a dōjō. Resigned, he meant to spend the rest of his life as an anachronism. He even considered destroying his secret scrolls and weapons before he died, so that the techniques would not fall into the wrong hands.

Ishitani wanted to disappear, and he found the place for it in a job as a night watchman. The match factory in Kōbe he guarded belonged to the Takamatsu family. There he met the family’s young son, Takamatsu Toshitsugu. In a roundabout way Ishitani learned that the boy was already studying the _koppō jutsu_ (骨法術) of the Kotō-Ryū (虎倒流) and the _daken taijutsu_ (打拳体術) of the Shinden-Fudō-Ryū (神伝不動流) – with his grandfather, Toda Shinryūken Masamitsu.

## Ishitani Trains Takamatsu

Ishitani offered to initiate the young Takamatsu into the secrets of the Kukishinden-Ryū. Takamatsu eagerly accepted and became his student.

The lessons followed a fixed order. First Takamatsu learned the eight parts of the _happō_ method, then the _hiken_ (秘剣) method, the secret sword techniques. Training uses the sword, the short sword, and the _jutte_ (十手).

Toshitsugu trained hard. After his teacher’s death he became the 27th grandmaster of the Kukishinden-Ryū.

## Toda Shinryūken Masamitsu

At the same time, Takamatsu was being taught by Toda Shinryūken Masamitsu, the 32nd grandmaster of the Togakure-Ryū (戸隠流). According to Bujinkan tradition, Toda had earlier been the sword instructor of the Tokugawa government and had taught the shōgun’s samurai the art of the sword.

At 21, Takamatsu was declared unfit for military service. The reason was a ruptured eardrum, an injury from one of the countless fights of his youth. Takamatsu decided to go to China.

## Takamatsu Toshitsugu Goes to China

Next to the adventures waiting in China, a career in the match factory lost all its appeal. Japan was in upheaval, and Takamatsu believed China offered him better opportunities. He crossed the country and had to use his fighting skills for real, again and again, against all kinds of opponents. More than once they saved his life.

The Chinese boxers he trained with gave him a nickname: the **Mongolian Tiger**.

After his years of wandering he returned to Japan and moved into the mountains. In their solitude he sought to perfect himself. At 30 he became a priest of Tendai _mikkyō_ (密教) on Mount Hiei near Kyōto. This knowledge took him deeper into the philosophy of the ninja and their esoteric secrets. Thus prepared, he finally set out for home for good and went on to become the 33rd grandmaster of Togakure-Ryū ninjutsu.

## Hatsumi Masaaki

Hatsumi Masaaki was seven when he began training in the martial arts, first with his father’s _bokutō_ (木刀). In his youth he tried every popular Japanese martial art and later earned master’s grades in karate, aikidō, and jūdō. Besides the martial arts, his great passion was the theater.

After the war, Hatsumi watched in dismay as the American occupation troops picked up jūdō techniques with speed and eagerness. With their size and natural athleticism, they learned in months what took the Japanese years. What was the point of a system in which others could outdo you by strength alone? Hatsumi was convinced that somewhere there had to be a true martial art, one that would let a fighter come out on top in any situation.

## Takamatsu Trains Hatsumi

The lead came from his _kobudō_ (古武道) instructor, who told him about a teacher named Takamatsu Toshitsugu from the town of Kashihara in the west of the Iga region. Hatsumi did not want a sport for keeping fit or a rigid system of lifeless _kata_ (型). He wanted a living art of war, and until then he had searched in vain for a teacher who could give it to him. So he set out and traveled all the way across Honshū.

By then Takamatsu was well past sixty, a veteran of countless fights. In the young man before him he met his future spiritual successor and the next grandmaster of ninjutsu.

For years Hatsumi went through hard training. His teacher was kind, but he had the hands of a tiger. In the end Hatsumi inherited the title of grandmaster of the nine Ryū that Takamatsu Toshitsugu had embodied.

Even under the worst torture, a ninja agent could not reveal who had sent them. They didn’t know. Behind this, according to tradition, lay a system the ninja developed to keep their organization secret: three ranks, each with its own specialty and its own responsibilities.

## Jōnin

At the head of a Ryū (流) stood the _jōnin_ (上忍). He directed every activity and decided which jobs his ninja carried out and at what price. In the larger Ryū the _jōnin_ was a wise man who was well informed about everything. He based his decisions on a philosophical understanding of the universe as a whole.

The true _jōnin_ stood for harmony. He helped the oppressed without expecting great honor in return, even when the odds were not on his side.

To leave himself as little exposed as possible, he hid his true identity from almost all of his agents. Those who didn’t know it could neither reveal it under torture nor sell it to rivals.

The system had a second advantage. The _jōnin_ could send several agents on the same mission independently of one another to make sure it succeeded. Each brought back a piece of the situation, and from all of them he assembled the complete picture.

Numerous criminal organizations use this system of invisibility today, for very similar reasons.

## Chūnin

Anyone who wanted to hire a ninja clan sent a messenger to a place where _chūnin_ (中忍) were known to be found: a remote area, a fishing village, a pleasure district. The _chūnin_ then made contact, in a different disguise every time.

The _chūnin_ passed the commission on to the _jōnin_, who weighed the motives behind it and had background information gathered before he accepted or refused. Mistrust ran both ways: the client usually had the ninja carry out a fake job first. Betrayal and intrigue were part of everyday life at the time.

The clans differed, too. Some ninja Ryū were loyal to particular houses, others supported whichever house pursued their own goals, and still others took any job as long as the fee was right. These lesser clans, often acting without the foresight of a _jōnin_, did much to give ninjutsu its bad reputation.

The _chūnin_ were middlemen, and a whole group of them worked for the _jōnin_. Their job was to interpret his words correctly and see that his orders were carried out properly. A _chūnin_ knew how to make an operation succeed and which agent to give which task. He also guaranteed the _jōnin_’s anonymity and safety.

As officers, the _chūnin_ rarely took part in an operation themselves. Their training did include combat and espionage techniques, but their specialties were strategy and effective leadership.

## Genin

All the fantastic legends grew up around the _genin_ (下忍). They were the agents who put the plans into action: in the end it was they who committed the murder, sabotaged the bridge, or opened a fortress’s gates to their own troops. On every mission they risked discovery, and they had to be able to survive on their own.

They had contact only with the _chūnin_ and usually did not know the _jōnin_, which kept his identity secret.

Between missions the _genin_ lived with their families in small, secret villages in mountains and swamps that were hard to reach. To the outside world they were farmers or fishermen. They trained in secret, and not even their neighbors knew about their deadly trade.

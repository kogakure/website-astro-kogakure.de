## The Civil War in Japan

The <q>Golden Age of Ninjutsu</q> lasted more than 400 years. It began with the rise of the samurai as a warrior caste and with the _bakufu_ (幕府), the shōgun’s seat of government in the Kamakura period (1185–1333). During this time, between 25 and 70 different Ryū (流, <q>schools</q>) arose, most of them in the provinces of Iga and Kōga. In the same period, Zen Buddhism came to Japan and became the philosophical foundation of the samurai.

The Hattori and Oe clans ruled Iga, while Kōga was ruled by more than 50 families.

In the 14th century, bitter fighting shook the country. In 1392 Japan was united under a single emperor. Only 75 years later, unrest broke out again. By then the local lords, the daimyō, had taken control. Their clans had built up power and influence over centuries, and the shōgun was reduced to a powerless puppet.

The daimyō fought each other for more than 120 years, until the general Toyotomi Hideyoshi finally reunited Japan in 1590. Throughout this time, ninja were needed everywhere. More and more ninja schools opened to meet the enormous demand.

## Firearms and Espionage

In 1543 merchants brought the first firearms to Japan: matchlock guns from Portugal. Among the samurai, using a _teppō_ (鉄砲, <q>arquebus</q>) was considered dishonorable. The task fell to the lowly _ashigaru_ (足軽), foot soldiers who were mostly peasants.

The ninja had no such scruples. They simply added the new firearms to their arsenal. From then on, wooden cannons, bronze pistols, and anti-personnel mines were part of their equipment.

Many generals hired ninja to spy on their rivals. Two of them were Takeda Shingen and his rival Uesugi Kenshin. Takeda’s spies sent their messages by signal fire, while Uesugi’s ninja often traveled disguised as itinerant doctors. The famous general Tokugawa Ieyasu, too, often relied on ninja from Iga and Kōga.

## Ninja Provinces and Their Rulers

Kōga was ruled by more than 50 different ninja clans. Iga, on the other hand, was divided among three long-established families: the Hattori, the Momochi, and the Fujibayashi.

The Momochi clan ruled the south of the province, the Hattori the center, and the Fujibayashi the north. The Fujibayashi could also call on ninja from Kōga.

Their commanders were Momochi Sandayū, Hattori Hanzō, and Fujibayashi Nagato. All three lived in the 16th century.

Momochi Sandayū kept three houses, each with a wife and children. This confused his enemies and protected his identity. Whenever an area became too dangerous for him, he simply changed his home and his identity.

In the 14th century, political and economic conditions grew more and more unstable, and many new Ryū were founded. When creative thinking and unconventional action were the only things left to secure survival, families had to swim against the current. Other Ryū existed only for a while: they helped a particular warlord, army, or religious order with a single matter and then disbanded.

Some styles stayed within one region and, for geopolitical reasons, allied themselves with the other ninjutsu schools there. Still others relied entirely on the experience, knowledge, and wisdom their founder had gathered over a lifetime.

The historically documented ninjutsu Ryū also differed widely in size and importance. Some clans consisted of a handful of family members, others of hundreds of ninja.

## The Most Important Schools

Some of the historically most significant ninjutsu Ryū, with their leaders, areas of operation, and military and political ties:

- **Nakagawa-Ryū**: organized by Nakagawa Kohayato, active in Aomori Prefecture.
- **Haguro-Ryū**: based in Yamagata Prefecture. According to tradition, it was developed by the warrior ascetics of Mount Haguro.
- **Uesugi-Ryū**: a military espionage organization that Usami Suruganokami Sadayuki (Niigata Prefecture) built for Uesugi Kenshin.
- **Kaji-Ryū**: founded by Kaji Ominokami Kagehide, a student of the Uesugi-Ryū’s founder. It also had ties to the founding fathers of the Hattori-Ryū in Iga Province.
- **Matsumoto-Ryū**: active in Tochigi Prefecture.
- **Matsuda-Ryū**: active in what is now Ibaraki.
- **Koyo-Ryū**, **Ninko-Ryū**, and **Takeda-Ryū**: founded by Takeda Shingen for his agents. Their spies often posed as wandering monks or traders.
- **Fūma-Ninpō**: founded by Fūma Kotarō in Kanagawa Prefecture, specialized in guerrilla warfare.
- **Akiba-Ryū** and **Ichizen-Ryū**: founded in Aichi Prefecture by Hachisuka Koroku Masakatsu, a famous warrior of the region.
- **Mino-Ryū**: developed in Gifu Prefecture during the rule of Saitō Dōsan. It included the Kurokawa ninja group from the Kōga area.
- **Echizen-Ryū**: founded in Fukui Prefecture by Iga ninja who had escaped Oda Nobunaga’s troops.
- **Yoshitsune-Ryū**: according to tradition developed by Minamoto Yoshitsune in Fukui Prefecture. The school combined the espionage methods of Ise Saburō with various teachings of the _yamabushi_ (山伏).
- **Kōga-Ryū** (甲賀流): a regional ninjutsu tradition of more than 50 families.
- **Iga-Ryū** (伊賀流): also a regional tradition, made up of several important families, among them the Hattori and Momochi clans.
- **Negoro-Ryū**, **Saiga-Ryū**, **Natori-Ryū**, and **Kishū-Ryū**: all had their home base in Wakayama Prefecture. The Negoro-Ryū was founded by the firearms expert Suginobō Myōsan. The Saiga-Ryū trained specialists in firearms and explosives. The Natori-Ryū was founded by Natori Sanjūrō Masatake, author of the ninjutsu reference work <cite>Shōninki</cite>.
- **Bizen-Ryū**: came from Okayama Prefecture.
- **Fukushima-Ryū**: developed by Nojirijiro Jirouemon Narimasa in Shimane Prefecture.
- **Kuroda-Ryū**: served the ruling Kuroda family in Fukuoka Prefecture.
- **Nanban-Ryū**: active in Nagasaki Prefecture.
- **Satsuma-Ninpō**: served the ruling Shimazu family in Kagoshima Prefecture.

With few exceptions, these Ryū had died out long before the Meiji era began in 1868.

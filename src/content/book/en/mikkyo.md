## In Tune With All Things

There is no such thing as coincidence – so the _mikkyō_ (密教) teaches. What we call good or bad luck are unchanneled forces showing themselves in our lives. To grasp reality in all its perfection, you therefore need the right state of mind. The ninja practiced _ninpō mikkyō_ (忍法密教), the tantric teachings of Buddhism, to become attuned to all things and turn into thoroughly natural beings.

## Ninja and Science

In a sense, the ninja were scientists. They wanted to understand how organisms work and how natural processes unfold. At the same time, they were drawn to deeper spiritual and occult phenomena. They trusted in their effects, which had proven themselves many times over the centuries since they first arose.

For many things, science today is not yet ready. Decoding DNA only scratches the surface. It will be a long time before the workings of mind and consciousness can perhaps one day be explained.

## Asian Arts and Modern Science

Many Asian arts, especially in medicine, were considered ridiculous until recently. Now that acupuncture, for one, is widely considered effective, an ever wider public is embracing these arts.

That settles nothing yet. How meridians and the dependencies inside the body work is still unclear, and the results rest on speculation and assumption.

## Openness to All Aspects

Today’s ninja are not closed to new ideas or to old ones. They are not bound to any particular religion or creed. From the religions they take whatever has value for them and build it into their own worldview as working principles.

All religious and philosophical aspects are therefore only suggestions. Holding certain beliefs in order to call oneself a ninja was never mandatory, and it should not be today.

A _kamiza_ (神座), a shrine in the dōjō, does not make a ninja. Only spirit and physical ability do, whether the ninja is Christian, Buddhist, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, or of an entirely different persuasion.

## Philosophy and Religion as Ethical Foundations

One thing, however, is certain: a ninja without a philosophical or religious foundation is a disgrace to all. Those who focus on physical development alone will fail. Anyone proud of smashing boards or beating opponents in tournaments has chosen the wrong art in Bujinkan Budō Taijutsu, or should never have chosen it. Kickboxing, karate, or better still boxing is the right choice for them.

Without this foundation, they will also never rise beyond a certain level in the Bujinkan: the 4th _dan_ (段).

## Origin of the Mikkyō in India

The roots of _mikkyō_ lie in the esoteric, tantric currents of India. These teachings originally came from Hinduism. As tantra, they entered the esoteric Buddhism of the Vajrayana, a movement that grew out of the Mahayana and is practiced today mainly in Tibet.

## Japanese Monks in China

In the Heian period, Emperor Kanmu sent two monks to China: Saichō (Dengyō Daishi) and Kūkai (Kōbō Daishi). They were to study Buddhism and so improve the religious situation in Japan. When they returned, they brought the foundations of _mikkyō_ with them.

In China, Kūkai studied Mizong. After his return, in 816, he founded the Kongōbuji monastery on Mount Kōya near Ōsaka, the headquarters of Shingon (真言), an esoteric Buddhist sect of _mikkyō_. His teaching united Shintō and Buddhism.

## Shingon

Shingon places special weight on the _sanmitsu_ (三密, <q>the three secrets</q>): thought, word, and deed can lead anyone to Buddhahood. The sect’s defining feature is the realization of a harmonious relationship among the five elements.

## Tendai

The second monk, Saichō, studied Tiantai, a school of Mahayana Buddhism. After his return, he founded the Enryakuji monastery on Mount Hiei for his sect, Tendai (天台). It became the most powerful monastery in Japan: at times 40,000 monks and _sōhei_ (僧兵), Buddhist warrior monks, stood under the protection of the imperial house.

Then Oda Nobunaga went to war against the Ikkō ikki, the rebel peasant warriors, against the _sōhei_, and against the ninja. In the course of this campaign he burned the monastery to the ground. By then Tendai had long been divided into three major currents: Sanmon and Jimon, which split as early as the late 10th century, and Shinsei.

The most important teaching of Tendai is to follow three precepts: <q>Avoid evil wherever it appears, do good wherever possible, and respect all living beings.</q>

## The Mikkyō of the Ninja

In the countries of its origin, the foundations of _mikkyō_ grew into a religion over the following centuries. A great number of gods were added and worshiped.

The ninja, by contrast, used _mikkyō_ to enhance their skills and powers. To them it was more than a religion: a set of working principles in which the laws of the universe operate.

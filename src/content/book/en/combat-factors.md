## Nagare

Without flow, techniques look choppy, as if each one were performed on its own, cut off from the next. That is why the principle of flowing action, _nagare_ (流れ), is one of the most important in training. Without it, you try to force a technique onto a situation with all your strength. With no fluid adaptation, all that is left is the strength of muscle and bone.

## Renzoku

Combining several techniques is called _renzoku_ (連続). The sequence and chaining of individual techniques turns them into one continuous movement. Only then does the individual technique become effective.

## Ritsudō

Closely tied to flowing action is _ritsudō_ (律動), rhythm. It is the interplay of two or more people: the course of a fight, the transition from one phase to the next, from attack to defense, from moving forward to moving back. The defender has to adapt to the attacker’s movements. The emotional state of both opponents is part of the rhythm too, and so is the tensing and relaxing of their muscles.

At its core, rhythm is the play of cause and effect in combat. An attack draws a defense, the defense draws another attack, until the fight is over.

## Undō

_Undō_ (運動) means movement. The term also covers technique and body movement. Body movement is divided into two areas:

- _shishi undō_ (四肢運動), movement of the limbs
- _tai sabaki_ (体捌き), movement of the trunk

## Kokyūhō

Many neglect it, yet breathing, _kokyūhō_ (呼吸法), is an important part of combat. Most adults breathe into the chest or even only through the throat. This way of breathing is unnatural.

> The true man breathes with his heels; the mass of men breathe with their throats.
>
> **Zhuangzi**

Children and <q>true</q> people breathe with the diaphragm, into the belly. This keeps the body calm and relaxed, and it massages and gently moves the organs. Most people breathe through the mouth, which is unhealthy. Both inhaling and exhaling should go through the nose, because it filters, moistens, and warms the air.

In abdominal breathing, the belly rises outward as you inhale and draws inward as you exhale.

<aside class="banner">

**Learning Abdominal Breathing**

In the past, abdominal breathing was taught with a simple exercise: you wrapped a cloth tightly around your belly.

That way you stay aware of your breathing all day and notice at once when you slip into chest breathing.

After a few days or weeks the cloth is no longer needed, because the body has grown used to abdominal breathing.

In a fight, breathing decides between victory and defeat. It is the key to tensing and relaxing correctly (or to cramping up), and to powerful and dynamic movement.

</aside>

## Maai

Distance, _maai_ (間合い), matters enormously in a fight. Getting too close to a skilled fighter is unwise. But if an opponent needs room to execute techniques, you move in close. Avoid specializing in techniques that limit your reach and with it your effectiveness. Shifting in unexpected directions gives you a tremendous advantage. It frees you from the primitive way of fighting: standing in one spot and only blocking.

## Kikaigaku

Mechanics, _kikaigaku_, covers the body’s most effective responses to an attack: footwork, sidestepping and fighting distance, the proper use of the body weapons, and effective techniques and strategies.

## Doteki

Dynamics, _doteki_ (動的), is the most effective application of mechanics. It includes the proper use of energy, rhythm and flow, strength and flexibility, the ratio of speeds, and the ability to feel an action and adapt to it.

## Ito

Intention, _ito_ (意図), is the clearly recognized goal: victory, to which mechanics and dynamics are wholly subordinated. It includes the mindset that allows you to use violent techniques, an unbridled will to win that carries you past fear and limits, and an attitude that does not treat victory as the only possible outcome but also reckons with defeat.

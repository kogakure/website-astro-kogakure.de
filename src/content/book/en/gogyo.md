## The Theory of the Five Elements

Wood gives rise to fire, fire leaves ash, and water puts the fire out. The **_gogyō_** (五行), which comes from the Daoist teachings of China, describes transformations like these. The _godai_ (五大) system assigns every phenomenon to a fixed plane. The _gogyō_, by contrast, builds on the polarity theory of yin and yang, _in_ (陰) and _yō_ (陽) in Japanese, and seeks to define the cycles of transformation that take place without pause on the material plane.

For this, five code designations were developed. They are meant as a code, not as a literal classification:

- **_Sui_ (水, water):** dissolving
- **_Moku_ (木, wood):** growing
- **_Ka_ (火, fire):** evaporating
- **_Do_ (土, earth):** condensing
- **_Kin_ (金, metal):** hardening

In ancient China, everything was subject to an eternal cycle: the seasons, the days, the processes in the body. The human being was no isolated creature but a small universe in its own right, a microcosm within the macrocosm. No part of Chinese culture could escape this theory, whether politics, medicine, art, or martial arts. That is why organs, cardinal directions, colors, seasons, animals, and much else were assigned to the five elements.

## Interdependence of the Elements

The elements follow a fixed hierarchy. In one order, each element brings forth the next; in another, each destroys or subdues another. In the opposite order, one element overwhelms the other.

Wood produces fire, fire produces earth (as ash), earth produces metal (as ore), metal produces water (as condensation on its surface), and water, finally, produces wood (as plants).

Wood conquers earth as a plow. Earth tames water as a dam. Water quenches fire, fire melts metal, and metal fells wood as an ax or a saw.

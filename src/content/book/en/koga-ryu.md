## Founding of the Kōga-Ryū

Besides Iga, one other region produced a ninja clan of comparable importance: Kōga. The Kōga-Ryū (甲賀流) was founded by 53 families, probably in the Tengyō era between 938 and 947.

It began with a war. Mochizuki Saburō Kaneie distinguished himself in the fighting against Taira no Masakado and was rewarded with a piece of land in the southeast of Ōmi Province. The area was called Kōga Gun, and Mochizuki took its name, calling himself Kōga Oni no Kami Kaneie from then on. The actual founder of the Kōga-Ryū, though, is said to be his son Oni no Kami Iechika, gifted in the military arts and in literature alike. According to tradition, he learned _genjutsu_ (幻術, <q>the art of illusion</q>) from Tatsumaki Hoshi, a Buddhist monk who lived in the area.

For seven generations the tradition stayed in the family: Oni no Kami Ienari, Iesada, Ienaga, Iekiyo, Ietō, Ieyoshi, and Yoshiyasu. Then it spread to other families – Mochizuki, Ugai, Naiki, and Akutagawa. During the Namboku War (1335–1395), scattered troops from the north and south of the realm joined these five families. With the help of the Kōga-Ryū, the alliance grew to 53 families. Among them were:

Kōga-Ryū, Shinpi-Ryū, Taira-Ryū, Isshu-Ryū, Byaku-Ryū, Tengu-Ryū, Taro-Ryū, Kuruya-Ryū, Tomo-Ryū, Tatara-Ryū, Sugawara-Ryū, Otomo-Ryū, Hiryū-Ryū, Fujiwara-Ryū, Sasaki-Ryū, Tachibana-Ryū, Kawachi-Yon-Tengu-Ryū.

## The Leading Families

The larger the Kōga-Ryū grew, the more clearly a leadership emerged. Eight families controlled the other Ryū in Kōga: Kōga, Mochizuki, Ugai, Naiki, Akutagawa, Ueno, Ban, and Nagano. Yet other groups in Kōga had highly capable masters as well, among them the Hiryūgumi, the Kakuryūgumi, the Tachibana Hachitengu Gumi, and the Kawachi Yon Tengu Gumi.

In the mid-15th century (1441–1451), according to tradition, the clan was led by Kōga Saburo, Mochizuki Goro, Ugai Ryūhoshi, Naiki Fujibe, and Akutagawa Kazuma. In the Bunmei period (1469–1487), the leaders were Kōga Saburo II, Mochizuki Yajiro, Ugai Chiaki, Naiki Gohei, and Akutagawa Tenpei. They were recruited by the Sasaki family, the daimyō (<q>great name</q>, a wealthy landowner) of the area, to lead its troops against the shōgun Ashikaga Yoshihisa.

Almost 100 years later, in 1570, the Sasaki turned to the ninja again, this time from both Kōga and Iga. They also brought in samurai from Kōga. Their goal: to destroy Oda Nobunaga. The samurai went through intensive training in a short time so they could carry out the combat strategies of the _jōnin_ (上忍).

The Sasaki army marched in three divisions. The first was led by ninja of the Mikumo-Ryū, Takanose-Ryū, Mizuhara-Ryū, and Inui-Ryū, the second by the other 53 Kōga families, the third by the Sasaki samurai. In the middle of the battle, Mikumo Iyo Kami, who commanded one of the armies, changed sides and attacked the Sasaki army from the rear. The battle was lost, and Sasaki himself barely escaped.

## Kōga Ninja in the Service of Tokugawa Ieyasu

In his struggle for power over Japan, Tokugawa Ieyasu needed time. His army had to assemble in the east for the Battle of Sekigahara, the final decisive battle of the civil war. Fushimi Castle near Kyōto was to buy him that time: besieged by the armies of the west, it had to hold out until the Tokugawa forces were ready.

400 ninja from Kōga helped defend it. Some fought inside the castle; the others harassed the enemy outside with attacks of every kind. About 100 of them died. After Tokugawa’s victory, the Kōga ninja held a ceremony, and it is said that Mochizuki and Arakawa were among the dead.

## The Shimabara Rebellion

One of the last missions of the Kōga ninja came during the **Shimabara no ran**, the rebellion of the Christians. Christian samurai had risen up and occupied Hara Castle on the Shimabara Peninsula (Hizen Province) on Kyūshū. Matsudaira Izu no Kami Nobutsuna sent ten ninja from Kōga to gather information for the shōgun’s samurai and prepare the attack on the castle.

They were led by two veterans: Mochizuki Heidayu, 63 years old, and Akutagawa Kiyouemon, 60 years old. Both had fought in the Battle of **Sekigahara**. With them came:

- Iwane Kanbei, 56 years old
- Ban Gohei, 53 years old
- Natsumi Kakusuke, 41 years old
- Akutagawa Shichirobei, 25 years old
- Kamogai Kanuemon, 56 years old
- Iwane Kanzaemon, 45 years old
- Mochizuki Yoemon, 33 years old
- Yamanaka Jutayu, 24 years old

They arrived on January 4, 1638. Their first task: a map of the area around the castle. Just 15 days later, a detailed map of the castle and the enemy forces went to Edo, present-day Tōkyō, to the shōgun Tokugawa Iemitsu.

The ninja from Kōga were also called _ongyō no mono_ (<q>hidden persons</q>). It is said that they slipped into the castle every night without trouble. On January 21, with their own supplies running low, they stole food from inside – and picked up some secret passwords along the way.

On January 27, five of them got into the castle disguised as soldiers: Mochizuki Yoemon, Akutagawa Shichirobei, Natsumi Kakusuke, Yamanaka Jutayu, and Ban Gohei. The besiegers fired their guns, and the defenders put out their torches so as not to offer a target. Late at night, when the guards grew careless, the five climbed over the walls under cover of darkness.

Then Akutagawa, not watching his step, fell into a hole. Mochizuki rushed to help him, but the noise alerted the guards. They relit the torches, and the intruders were discovered. Mochizuki and Akutagawa ran straight through the troops, snatched one of the Christian banners on the way, and came under fire. All five escaped, though Mochizuki and Akutagawa were wounded.

When the castle was stormed on February 24, the Kōga ninja served as liaisons between the units. Miyamoto Musashi, the most famous swordmaster in Japan, is also said to have helped the shōgun plan the battle. A stone thrown from the wall by a woman struck him and forced him to withdraw. Afterward Musashi complained about the loss of his youthful strength.

## The Last Kōga Ninja

The Kōga-Ryū is said to have survived until the middle of the 20th century – in the person of Fujita Seiko (1898–1966). He is said to have been the 14th _sōke_ (宗家) of the Kōga-Ryū. In World War II he led small units in the jungle. At the 1964 Olympic Games in Tōkyō, he tried to persuade athletes to train with ninja methods and drew attention with spectacular demonstrations.

Fujita Seiko died of cirrhosis of the liver at his home in Tōkyō on January 4, 1966. He was grandmaster of Kōga-Ninja-Ryū-Jūjutsu and the 14th _sōke_ of Nanban Satto-Ryū _kenpō_ (拳法). According to official information, he never passed on a license to teach the Kōga-Ryū to any student.

There is a book titled <cite>Ninjutsu no Gokui</cite>. Its author, Gingetsu, learned the techniques and history of the Kōga-Ryū from Tanemura Ihachiro, a _jōnin_ of the school. The techniques the book describes closely resemble those of the Togakure-Ryū from Iga.

## The School of the Tall Tree and the Willow Spirit

### Unryū, the Mountain Priest

In 1569, during the Eiroku era (1558–1570), a mountain priest from the Abe family lived in the Funagata Yama area of Miyagi. People called him Unryū (<q>cloud dragon</q>); the <cite>Bugei Ryūha Daijiten</cite> records his name as Sounryu. Unryū was an expert in _shuriken_ (手裏剣), _bōjutsu_ (棒術), _yari_ (槍), _naginata_ (薙刀), and the _taijutsu_ (体術) of the <cite>Amatsu Tatara Rinpo Hiden Makimono</cite>.

The Amatsu Tatara scrolls were kept by the Abe, Nakatomi, Ōtomo, and Mononobe families. Takamatsu Sensei’s family also owned a copy, because it was related to the Kuki family.

Unryū passed his system on to Ito Sukesada, a famous martial artist of his day. Ito was a samurai from Fukushima in the service of Katakura Kojūrō. He added _hanbō_ (半棒), _kenjutsu_ (剣術), and _kodachi_ (小太刀) to Unryū’s teachings.

### Takagi Oriemon Shigenobu

Ito taught the techniques that would later become the Takagi-Yōshin-Ryū to a young samurai from Tōhoku-Shiroishi-Han in Oku: Takagi Oriemon Shigenobu. He was born on April 2, 1625, and died on October 7, 1711. He received the _menkyo kaiden_ (免許皆伝) when he was just 20 years old. According to tradition, Emperor Higashiyama appointed him _shihan_ (師範) of the imperial bodyguard in six different martial arts on August 15, 1695.

He changed, improved, and expanded what he had learned from Ito. He gathered it into a school and named it after himself: **Takagi-Yōshin-Ryū** (高木楊心流).

Takagi trained relentlessly to better himself, because he wanted to avenge his father’s murder. His father had left him a valuable lesson: <q>A willow is flexible, but a tall tree is fragile.</q>

### Names and Influences

Over the course of its history, the style has gone by several names, among them _jū tai jutsu_ (柔体術), _jūjutsu_ (柔術), and _daken taijutsu_ (打拳体術). It was strongly influenced by the _jūjutsu_ of the Takenouchi-Ryū and by the Kukishin-Ryū.

In the 17th century, two _sōke_ (宗家) met in a contest: Takagi Gennoshin Hideshige of the Takagi-Yōshin-Ryū and Ōkuni Kihei Shigenobu of the Kukishin-Ryū. The contest turned into a close friendship. The two systems exchanged knowledge and were restructured as a result.

In August 1908, Mizuta Tadafusa Yoshitarō passed the Ryū on to Takamatsu Toshitsugu, and in May 1959 it went to Hatsumi Masaaki.

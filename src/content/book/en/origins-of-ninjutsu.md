## Demons of the Forest

<img src="/images/historical/ushiwakamaru-to-tengu.webp" alt="Night in a forest of huge cedars under a full moon: a white-bearded, winged tengu with a long red nose holds a sword and directs three young men practicing with wooden swords, while more tengu watch from the mist.">
<figcaption>Ushiwakamaru and the <em>tengu</em> (牛若丸と天狗)</figcaption>
</figure>

Deep in the cursed forests of Japan, so the stories went, lived the _tengu_ (天狗, <q>sky dog</q>): winged, long-nosed mythical creatures, demons of the forest from Japanese folklore.

Frightened villagers told each other stories about the ninja. The _tengu_, they said, were the ninja’s ancestors and had taught them their martial arts. Ninja were held to be mages, sorcerers, demons, immortal and invulnerable. They could walk through walls, fly, and vanish whenever they pleased. No one could defeat them.

The ninja _enjoyed_ this reputation. It kept the curious away and protected their anonymity.

Young samurai facing a ninja for the first time often did not survive. Not because the ninja fought better: their own fear paralyzed them, and superstition did the rest.

## Origins in China

<figure>
<img src="/images/historical/sonshi.webp" alt="Chinese men in plain robes walk in single file along a path through a misty conifer forest; one carries a rolled bamboo book in his arm, the man in front leans on a staff.">
<figcaption>A bamboo book of Sun Tzu (孫子)</figcaption>
</figure>

Much about the beginnings of ninjutsu is still uncertain. Its very first roots most likely lie on the Chinese mainland, and refugees brought the ideas from there to Japan.

One argument for this is that ninjutsu supposedly also existed in Korea and China, where it was known as Lin Kuei.

One of the first works on espionage is probably the more than 2000-year-old military classic <cite>The Art of War</cite>, which tradition ascribes to **Sun Tzu**. The text reached Japan early and probably became part of early ninjutsu. Influences from India and Tibet found their way in as well, such as the _kuji-in_ (九字印), _mantra_ (真言), _mikkyō_ (密教), and _mandala_ (曼荼羅).

## The First _Shinobi_

According to tradition, the prince regent Shōtoku Taishi (574–622 AD) was the first to use agents to learn what really lay behind disputes. The name ninjutsu, or _shinobi no jutsu_ (忍びの術), is also said to have come into being during his rule, though no contemporary source records it.

One of Shōtoku’s generals, Ōtomo no Saijin, is said to have sent out scouts and used their information to win several great victories. Shōtoku Taishi then reportedly named him _shinobi_ (忍び), which means roughly <q>to hide</q> or <q>to camouflage oneself</q>.

At first, ninjutsu was probably nothing more than scouting. Only during the Heian period (794–1185) did it grow into a deadly art.

## Ninjutsu and Buddhism

According to tradition, after Shōtoku’s death a cruel conflict broke out in Japan between Buddhists and followers of Shintō. Each side wanted its teaching to become the new state religion, and the conflict ended in civil war. A _yamabushi_ (山伏) named En no Gyōja (役行者) stepped into public view. Through a new branch of Buddhism, _shugendō_ (修験道), he set out to restore order. The new religion won wide support among the people. The nobility feared that the _yamabushi_ might seize the power of the state and raised a large army against them.

Facing this superior force, the priests withdrew into the mountains around Kyōto. There they carried on their war according to Chinese military tactics.

## The Warriors of the Mountains

Throughout the years of persecution, the _yamabushi_ slipped away from the troops again and again, and astonishing stories were told about them.

They walked over glowing coals and plunged their hands into boiling water without being burned. They put out sudden fires and brought cold water to a boil in seconds. They could bind an enemy’s sword so tightly in its _saya_ (鞘, <q>scabbard</q>) that no force could draw it. This _shugendō_, the way of miraculous achievement through asceticism, protected the warrior monks from the troops.

From the _yamabushi_ the ninja learned the _mudrā_ (印相, <q>seal</q> or <q>sign</q>), a series of 81 finger gestures said to control specific areas of the psyche. With them the ninja mastered feelings such as fear, hunger, cold, and pain, sharpened their senses, and focused their minds. The _mudrā_ come from India. Each one is the physical expression of a particular state of consciousness.

Ninjutsu used only nine of these gestures. They are called _kuji-in_, the nine basic gestures of the hand.

In the Heian period (794–1185), _onmyōdō_ (陰陽道, <q>divination and astrology</q>) flourished in Japan and took ninjutsu another step forward. _Onmyōdō_ was an ancient, comprehensive science built from elements of Chinese divination and astrology.

## The Role of the Ninja in the Middle Ages

By the Middle Ages, ninjutsu was slowly taking a different direction. Fewer and fewer students learned the deep foundations of _shugendō_ and the other sciences. It often took a whole lifetime to come close to perfecting even one of these techniques.

Many lords kept close ties with the warriors of the mountains, among them the powerful Genji family (another name for the Minamoto). More and more often, the ninja were hired for smaller reconnaissance missions. At this point, the terms ninja and ninjutsu were not yet in use. It took some time before the members of these clans saw themselves as ninja.

To improve ninjutsu and train agents faster, new elements were added. The older arts, which took long and laborious work to learn, gradually gave way to physical and psychological feats, optical illusions, and sleight of hand.

Refined techniques, an efficient martial art tested for its lethality, and control of the body made the ninja nearly invincible fighters. Their reputation spread fear and terror through the ranks of their enemies.

Ninjutsu was not firmly established until the middle of the Heian period.

In Iga Province, the Hattori family laid the foundation for their Iga ninjutsu school.

## The Great Campaign Against Iga

For two years Oda Nobunaga had other matters to deal with before he could turn to Iga. Then two samurai offered him their services as advisors and guides: Fukuchi Iyo, from a village above Tsuge in Iga, and his comrade Mimisu Iyajiro. Nobunaga saw his chance to finally subdue Iga, which refused to accept his supremacy. He planned a far larger campaign than the one his son Oda Nobuo had lost in 1579.

Nobunaga’s armies were to attack from six sides at once. Fukuchi Iyo and Mimisu Iyajiro would advance from the north through Kōga Province, because that route was the hardest to defend.

In August 1581, Nobunaga himself set out from Azuchi at the head of his forces. But after half a day’s march he fell ill and had to call off the operation. By September he had recovered and summoned his generals. His plan: burn and lay waste to as much as possible, so that the people of Iga could not use their tactics.

Iga learned of the six armies in time. But it had nothing to set against a force of that size.

## Six Routes into Iga

Iga had about 4,000 soldiers. The defenders decided to concentrate their forces in two places. One was the Heirakuji temple on a small hill in the village of Ueno, where Iga Ueno Castle stands today. The other was Mount Tendoyama, not far from where Maruyama Castle had once stood. Nobunaga’s troops advanced along six routes:

### Iseji

The Iseji route was commanded by Oda Nobuo, also known as Kitabatake Chujo Nobuo. At his side rode Yoshida Goro and Oda Shichibei no Jo Nobuzumi with more than 10,000 soldiers and mounted samurai. With him came everyone who had taken part in the 1579 invasion, among them Takigawa Saburohei Kazumasu, Nagano Sakyo, and Hioki Daizenryo – this time united in one army. After a swift advance, Nobuo split his army into three to attack the villages and forts where refugees from Iga were sheltering. Takigawa besieged the fortresses of Tanenama no Sho and Kunimiyama, while Nagano and Hioki attacked the Ao Valley and the fortress at Kashiwao. Nobuo met little resistance. But the besieged harassed his troops again and again and attacked them at night.

### Tsuge

More than 12,000 soldiers came from the north through the Kōga area. They marched past Tsuge near the border and on to the south. The army was led by Niwa Gorozaemon Nagahide, Takigawa Sakon Shogen, Takigawa Yoshidaou, Wakabe Sakyo no Shinjuko, and Todo Shogen. Ninja raided Niwa Nagahide’s camp at every opportunity. In the end, no one there dared to sleep for fear of never waking up again.

### Tamataki

Over 7,000 soldiers also came from the north through the Kōga area, but took the western route through the village of Tamataki. They were commanded by Gamo Hida no Kami Shikyo, Wakisaka Jinnai Yasuharu, and Yamaoka Shukei Gashira. <q>Shukei Gashira</q> is not a name; it means <q>paymaster</q>. The army is said to have met resistance on its way through Kōga. Apparently a member of the Mochizuki family, Mochizuki Chotaro, fought a duel with a man named Yamauchi Zaemon Dono. One reason may have been the alliance between the families of Iga and Kōga.

### Tarao

From the northwest came Hori Kyūtarō Hidemasa and Tarao Shirobei Mitsuhiro with 2,300 men. The people of northwestern Iga chose to defend Kannoji, a Buddhist temple on Mount Hijiyama west of Ueno.

### Yamato Hase

According to tradition, 10,000 soldiers attacked from Yamato Province in the south, led by Asano Yahei Nagamasa (浅野長政), Shinjo Suruga no Kami, Ikoma Gagaku Kashira, Mori Iki no Kami, Toda Danzo Shoshitsu, Sawabara Jiro, Akiyama Sanou Tayu, and Yoshino no Miya Naishoyu. They besieged Kashiwabara. In command there was Takano, a samurai and a master of the night attack. He relied on old methods, those of Kusunoki Masashige, the founder of the Kusunoki-Ryū. One of them: women and children waved torches. It looked like an attack was about to begin and spread fear and disorder in the enemy camps.

### Kasama

Tsutsui Junke and his nephew Tsutsui Shiro Sadatsugu also came from Yamato in the south, but by way of Kasama, farther north than Asano Nagamasa’s army. Their 3,000 soldiers did not besiege Kashiwabara, however. They moved north to help Gamo Ujisato besiege Hijiyama.

## The Battle for Hijiyama

On the 27th day of the ninth lunar month, Gamo Ujisato, Wakisaka, Yamaoka, and Tsutsui attacked Hijiyama together. According to tradition, small groups of ninja lay hidden along the slope – the same strategy that had worked against Nobuo’s invasion. They fell on Gamo’s men as they climbed. By the time the troops reached the summit, they were badly mauled and had no fight left in them.

Two men from Iga, Momoda Tobei and Yokoyama Jinsuke, made it to the foot of the mountain and took the heads of Gamo’s two sons. The besiegers broke off the attack at once and withdrew. Momoda Tobei, Fukukita Shogen, Mori Shirozaemon, Machii Seibei, Yokoyama Jinsuke, Yamada Kanshiro, and one other man won great honor for this success. They were called the <q>Seven Spears</q>.

The forces of Iga kept up their night raids. They prepared a swift strike on Tsutsui’s army and caught it by surprise. His soldiers did not even have time to put on their armor, and a gust of wind blew out all the torches. In the darkness, Tsutsui’s soldiers cut each other down. The Iga troops, by contrast, used code words to tell friend from foe. A second attack on Tsutsui’s army succeeded as well.

## The Defeat of the Insurgents

Once Nobunaga’s armies had beaten back all resistance, they gathered around Hijiyama. Thirty thousand soldiers of his once 44,300-strong army surrounded the mountain. Despite this overwhelming advantage, Nobunaga could not win the fight by force of arms. The weather was dry and the wind strong, so he had the entire area set on fire. Anyone who tried to escape the flames was driven back.

That was the end of the last great uprising in Iga. Those who kept fighting Oda scattered across the region. When exactly Hijiyama fell is unclear; the sources give different dates in the autumn of 1581. Nobunaga’s forces hunted down the last resisters, and only in 1582 was all resistance crushed.

## The Survivors

Little was left of Iga’s once 4,000-strong army, and many innocent people died in Nobunaga’s furious hunt for ninja. Most people knew about the ninja, but only a minority belonged to the clans or were trained in ninjutsu. Some sources report that only eight survivors were left of the three Hattori clans. Many fled to other parts of Japan. A _jōnin_ (上忍) of the Hattori family was killed when Tsutsui attacked Kikyo Castle.

A last attempt on Nobunaga’s life was made by Kido, Harada, and Jindai. Nobunaga was traveling through Iga to see the results of his invasion and reached a place called Ichinomya. There the three had set up three cannons, each from a different direction. They opened fire on the spot where Nobunaga sat with his retinue. Nobunaga survived; seven or eight of his followers did not. Kido, Harada, and Jindai escaped into the woods.

Momochi Sandayū, a well-known _jōnin_, also escaped Nobunaga’s troops with some of his men. They fled to Ryūguchi near Uda Sanbonmatsu in Yamato Province. There they stayed until June 1582, although his men wanted to keep fighting. Then word reached them that Oda Nobunaga had been assassinated by his own general, Akechi Mitsuhide.

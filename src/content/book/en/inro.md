A few lacquered compartments on a cord at the belt: the _inrō_ (印籠) was the ninja’s travel pharmacy. With what it held, the ninja mixed poisons and medicines.

## Poisons

### To Kill, Paralyze, or Stun

The ninja knew how to mix poisons and drugs, and extracted them from plants, animals, and minerals.

<figure>
<img src="/images/article/inro-02-preparing-medicine-and-poison.webp" alt="A shinobi kneels in a dim hut and grinds roots in a stone mortar.">
<figcaption>Preparing medicine and poison</figcaption>
</figure>

A poison could kill an opponent. Far more often, though, the ninja used it to paralyze, stun, or incapacitate. For the mission, it mattered how fast a poison worked: sometimes an opponent had to be silenced at once; at other times the ninja wanted to be safely away before the effect set in.

Poison was smeared on weapons, mixed into food and drink, or released as gas. Even flowers are said to have been poisoned.

### Recipes

The simplest method needed nothing from the pharmacy. Before an operation, the ninja dragged the weapons through horse manure. Anyone wounded by them often developed muscle cramps soon after and died of blood poisoning.

For a strange poison called _gyokuro_ (玉露), green tea was buried in the ground for 30 to 40 days and then mixed with soybean paste. A sick person given this drink died within a few days. A healthy person could last a month, but after 70 days they too died of its effects.

_Zagarashijaku_, a poison made from green plums or peaches, was known for killing quickly.

### Sleeping Gases

The ninja also used poison gas. Mostly it did not kill but only stunned: a little of it in the air, and the guards grew sluggish or fell asleep. The ninja could then finish the job undisturbed.

For one of these sleeping gases, the blood of a newt, a snake, and a mole was mixed, and a sheet of paper soaked up the tincture. When the paper was burned, everyone who breathed the smoke fell asleep.

Another was made from three powdered male rats, a few leaves of the paulownia tree, a centipede, and a handful of cottonseeds, pressed into a ball. When the ball was burned, anyone who inhaled the fumes fell into a deep sleep.

### Paralysis, Itching, Laughter

The ninja made a liquid narcotic from ground hemp leaves mixed into tea. To paralyze an opponent, the ninja often used the bodily fluids of toads, pufferfish, or other poisonous animals.

If a pursuer only had to be held up, an itching powder made from stinging nettles was enough. There is also said to have been a mushroom that sent whoever ate it into a fit of laughter, making the victim easy to overpower.

One terrible poison drove its victim insane. Few people today know how to make it. Only this much can be made public: five to ten seeds of a plant were ground to powder and mixed into the food.

### Poisonous Plants

Strong poisonous plants grew in Japan, such as aconite (_torikabuto_, 鳥兜), whose root is regarded as Japan’s classic weapon poison, water hemlock (_dokuzeri_, 毒芹), Japanese coriaria (_dokuutsugi_, 毒空木), Japanese scopolia (_hashiridokoro_, 走野老), Japanese star anise (_shikimi_, 樒), and Japanese andromeda (_asebi_, 馬酔木).

Many poisonous plants are also common in Central Europe, and some of them did not grow in Japan in the ninja’s time. They include arum, broom, henbane, bean, Christmas rose, yew, aconite, foxglove, spotted hemlock, false hellebore, wallflower, laburnum, autumn crocus, fool’s parsley, potato, arborvitae, lupine, lily of the valley, oleander, spindle tree, castor bean, red bryony, savin juniper, meadow buttercup, greater celandine, mezereon, thorn apple, deadly nightshade, water hemlock, and cypress spurge.

Many of them are fairly harmless on their own. With the right pharmacological knowledge and the right mixture, though, they yield dangerous poisons.

### The Dose Makes the Poison

In the right amount, any food is a poison. And in small doses, poison can even serve as medicine, as it does in homeopathy.

No one can say for certain how many people have been poisoned over the centuries. It certainly happened often, because poison used to be a reliable way to change a line of succession or sway politics. Medical examinations were poor, and only rarely did clear signs give a poisoning away.

Even today, forensic doctors assume that a fair number of deaths are caused by poisoning. It usually goes undetected when the deceased was elderly, because then no forensic examination is made. Heirs have probably killed people they wanted out of the way slowly and undetectably with small doses over a long period.

## Remedies and Healing Methods

### Wounds and Injuries

On a mission, the ninja was usually alone for a long time and had to provide their own medical care, treating minor and major injuries, poisoning, and other ailments.

There was a remedy for bleeding of every size. For small scratches, the ninja stopped the blood with the smoke of smoldering scraps of cloth. A large, bleeding wound was covered with chewed tea leaves and dried, powdered paulownia leaves. Japanese acacia, mugwort, peonies, and thuja also stopped bleeding.

Sword wounds were treated with a mixture of goutweed root and black cowpea, sometimes with crushed narcissus roots. Crushed leeks went on gunshot wounds. A paste of wheat flour and water soothed cuts from bamboo, above all the pain. Aconite, calamus, hemp, and chrysanthemums served as painkillers.

A mixture of tannin and ink soothed scalded skin. For sprains and strains, the ninja took Amur cork tree (_Phellodendron amurense_), half crushed and half raw, mixed it with vinegar, and applied it to the skin. Against wound infection, a piece of fish skin was laid on the wound and changed from time to time.

### Other Cures

After eating spoiled food, the ninja ate the same food again, this time charred.

The ninja are said to have been able to fight cancer. A branch of an old wisteria, ideally one with a growth on it, was ground to powder, mixed with water, and drunk. Three grams a day of water caltrop seed powder or water chestnut powder are also said to have served as a cancer cure.

Some ninja mastered the art of resuscitation. In Japanese _shiatsu_ (指圧), massage and stimulation of certain points on the body relieve or cure fainting and other ailments.

## Food

On every mission, the ninja carried an emergency ration: dried rice (_hoshii_, 糒), dried plums (_umeboshi_), and food pills such as _hyōrōgan_ (兵糧丸), _kikatsugan_ (飢渇丸) against hunger, and _suikatsugan_ (水渇丸) against thirst. The pills were made from rice and other starches such as kudzu or dried yam, with additions such as buckwheat, pine nuts, or plum flesh.

Peppermint powder and salted plums quenched thirst. When the water ran out, a certain kind of leek would do. Thirty sesame seeds are said to have brought brief relief from thirst.

Otherwise, the ninja ate what was normal and plentiful where they lived: bean paste, buckwheat flour, fish, vegetables, plums, sesame, tofu, unpolished rice, and other foods of the Japanese diet.

For a non-Japanese, this diet is hard to keep to and takes getting used to. But there is no need to. Hatsumi Masaaki once said that diets differ from country to country. What matters is to eat a balanced diet and not too much meat.

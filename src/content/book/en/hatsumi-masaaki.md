## Birth

On December 2, 1931, a boy named Hatsumi Yoshiaki was born in Noda in Chiba Prefecture. Only later did he change his given name to Masaaki. Dr. Hatsumi Masaaki is also known as Toratsugu, Tetsuzan, and Hisamune.

At seven, Hatsumi learned kendō from his father. He also trained in aikidō, jūdō, and karate. At school he did gymnastics and boxing and was captain of the soccer team. He also danced, which later helped him with budō. In high school he kept boxing and practiced jūdō. He also studied dramaturgy and theater.

## Theater Studies and Osteopathy

At Meiji University in Tōkyō, Hatsumi took his degree in theater studies and osteopathy. He then opened an osteopathic practice. While still a student he reached 4th _dan_ (段) in jūdō, which was rare at his age.

One day Hatsumi was asked whether he would train jūdō with American soldiers at the American base at Yokota. The Americans were taller and stronger. What took a Japanese man many years of hard practice, they mastered in a few months. Hatsumi began to have doubts: what good was jūdō if a smaller, weaker fighter could not use it to beat bigger, stronger opponents?

He set out in search of real martial arts and trained in _kobujutsu jūhappan_ under Ueno Sensei. After three years he had mastered the style, and his teacher said there was nothing more he could teach him. He advised Hatsumi to go to Nara and find a capable teacher there.

## Meeting Takamatsu

<img src="/images/historical/hatsumi-masaaki-02-spear.webp" alt="An older man in black stands in a deep lunge in a field, holding a long spear with a white tassel angled forward.">
<figcaption>Hatsumi Masaaki with the spear</figcaption>
</figure>

At 26, Hatsumi met Takamatsu Toshitsugu, who was called Mōko no Tora, the <q>Mongolian Tiger</q>.

From him he learned that in _taijutsu_ (体術), movement and distance can defeat a physically superior opponent.

From then on, for 15 years, Hatsumi traveled ten hours every week across the island of Honshū to his master. He left Noda on Friday night, spent the weekend with Takamatsu, and trained with him. On Sunday night he set off on the long journey home to open his practice again on Monday morning.

After Hatsumi passed the test of _sakki_ (殺気), Takamatsu gave him the _menkyo kaiden_ (免許皆伝) of the nine warrior traditions (_ryūha_, 流派).

## Hatsumi’s Sakki Test

Hatsumi tells how one day his master asked him to sit down and close his eyes. Then Takamatsu left the room. Hours passed. Suddenly Hatsumi felt a heavy, pressing force from behind, and in his mind’s eye he saw a body split in two. Without thinking, he rolled to the side. At that moment he heard his teacher say: <q>Well done, you did it, you can open your eyes now.</q> Takamatsu had crept up silently behind him with a sharp sword and struck.

## Takamatsu’s Death

A year later Takamatsu Sensei died. Before that, he told Hatsumi that he had taught him everything he knew, and so repaid his own masters for their kindness.

Takamatsu considered Hatsumi the most capable of his students and chose him to carry on his legacy. Hatsumi became the new _sōke_ (宗家).

## Founding of the Bujinkan

In honor of his teacher, Hatsumi named his _honbu_ (本部) dōjō the Bujinkan Dōjō. At first the dōjō was in his house. More recently he has built a new dōjō and now lives in a house in the countryside.

At 30 he married his wife, Mariko.

## Seminars Worldwide

Hatsumi used to travel around the world several times a year, holding seminars and _taikai_ (大会, <q>great gathering</q>, ninja events). Because of his advanced age, and to care for his wife, he now stays in Japan. He still trains now and then at the _honbu_ dōjō, the main training hall in Tōkyō.

## Awards and Honorary Degrees

Hatsumi has received numerous military and civic awards around the world. He earned two further doctorates, one in philosophy and one in science. In 1986 he received the <q>Black Belt’s Instructor of the Year Award</q>.

He is a scientist, painter, actor, musician, singer, and author. He has written countless columns for newspapers and magazines and many books on ninjutsu, and he has published the Bujinkan magazine <cite>Sanmyaku</cite> (山脈, <q>mountain range</q>).

He has made more than 25 videos and appeared in many films: <cite>Shinobi no Mono</cite>, the James Bond film <cite>You Only Live Twice</cite>, 50 episodes of the popular children’s series <cite>Jiraya</cite>, and <cite>Suteki na Mama</cite>.

In Japan he is regarded as a national treasure, and worldwide as one of the most important martial artists.

Because the nine schools are so complex, Hatsumi decided to teach them as a unit rather than separately.

Training under Hatsumi is said to have been very hard at first. In 1989, at the start of the Heisei era (Heisei means <q>becoming peaceful</q>), he adapted the Bujinkan to this idea and toned the training down.

Since 1995 the martial art of the Bujinkan has been called **Budō Taijutsu** throughout.

## Master of Nine Schools

Hatsumi is the legitimate successor of the nine _ryūha_:

- 34th _sōke_ – Togakure-Ryū (戸隠流) _ninpō_ (忍法)
- 28th _sōke_ – Gyokko-Ryū (玉虎流) _koshi jutsu_ (骨指術)
- 26th _sōke_ – Kukishinden-Ryū (九鬼神伝流) _happō hikenjutsu_ (八法秘剣術)
- 26th _sōke_ – Shinden-Fudō-Ryū (神伝不動流) _daken taijutsu_ (打拳体術)
- 21st _sōke_ – Gyokushin-Ryū (玉心流) _ninpō_
- 19th _sōke_ – Kotō-Ryū (虎倒流) _koppō jutsu_ (骨法術)
- 17th _sōke_ – Takagi-Yōshin-Ryū (高木楊心流) _jū tai jutsu_ (柔体術)
- 15th _sōke_ – Gikan-Ryū (義鑑流) _koppō jutsu_
- 14th _sōke_ – Kumogakure-Ryū (雲隠流) _ninpō_

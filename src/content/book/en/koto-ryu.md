## The School of Knocking Down the Tiger

### Origins

A Chinese warrior named Chan Busho is said to have brought the art to Japan by way of Korea. That is the common belief about where **Kotō-Ryū** (虎倒流) _koppō jutsu_ (骨法術) came from. If it is true, it happened many hundreds of years before the Kotō-Ryū was founded. The exact origin is unknown. According to the school’s tradition, in 1542 Sakagami Tarō Kunishige gathered the techniques into a _ryūha_ (流派). He was also the _sōke_ (宗家) of the Gyokko-Ryū (玉虎流), and the two styles have a great deal in common.

### Ishikawa Goemon

Many ninja and samurai trained in the methods of the Kotō-Ryū. According to legend, Momochi Sandayū taught Ishikawa Goemon, the infamous ninja and criminal. Momochi always denied that Ishikawa Goemon had ever been a member of his clan.

Goemon was seen as the <q>ninja Robin Hood</q>. He was probably caught and boiled to death in oil. Some, Takamatsu Toshitsugu among them, believe he was never caught at all and that the _bakufu_ (幕府) was simply too embarrassed to admit it. Often the story passed down by word of mouth is more credible than the written record.

### Fighting Style

The Kotō-Ryū contains the complete _kyūsho_ (急所), the pressure points for _atemi_ (当身). It also had a rare and unusual method of _kenjutsu_ (剣術): the swordsman held the sword as if unable to handle it, constantly changing grips and stances.

The school has a stance found nowhere else, _mangetsu no kamae_. You hold the sword above your head so that the sunlight on the blade blinds your opponent. When it rained, the blood groove of the blade caught water, and that too was used to blind the enemy.

<figure>
<img src="/images/article/koto-ryu-01-mangetsu-no-kamae.webp" alt="A swordsman holds his sword high above his head; sunlight flashes off the blade, and his opponent raises a hand to shield his eyes from the glare.">
<figcaption><em>Mangetsu no kamae</em></figcaption>
</figure>

Another specialty concerns the gaze. You don’t look your opponent in the eye but between the eyebrows. The opponent thinks you are making eye contact, yet cannot read your intentions, and that confuses them.

### Takamatsu Toshitsugu

Takamatsu Toshitsugu began training in the Kotō-Ryū at the age of nine. At 13 he was declared a master.

In 1960, Takamatsu is said to have convinced a journalist from the Tōkyō Sport newspaper of the Kotō-Ryū’s effectiveness: with his fingers, he drilled five holes into the bark of a tree.

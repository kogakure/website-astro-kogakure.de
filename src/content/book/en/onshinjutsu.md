## The Art of Hiding

Someone who is hidden can watch in relative safety or wait for the right moment. That makes hiding probably the most effective way to protect yourself. The art of hiding, concealing, camouflaging, and making yourself invisible is called **_onshin jutsu_** (隠身術), also _inton_ (隠遁) or _inpō_ (隠法). It falls into three areas:

- Reflecting no light, making no noise, and giving off no smell
- Disabling the opponent’s ability to see and hear
- Disguise and acting

It all starts with your own senses. To learn to hide, you first sharpen them and test how colors, shapes, sounds, distances, and smells work. Only then do you understand how these things connect and how to avoid standing out.

## The Five Methods of Inpō

On the foundation of _godai_ (五大), a principle developed that the ninja used for concealment. It closely resembles _gotonpō_ (五遁法). The areas cannot be strictly separated, because each one interacts directly with another.

### Chi – Method of Earth

- Using the earth
- Hiding in holes, hollows, and rock crevices, or behind stones (_uzura gakure no jutsu_, <q>the art of hiding like a quail</q>)
- Using plants for camouflage

### Sui – Method of Water

- Hiding in or under water
- Hiding behind artificial reed islands

### Ka – Method of Fire

- Camouflage using light, shadow, and sound
- Moving silently
- Watching for cast shadows and reflections
- Using dusk, fog, or rain for camouflage

### Fū – Method of Air

- Hiding in high places such as trees or poles
- Hiding in treetops
- Hiding on gables

### Kū – Method of Emptiness

- Camouflage when no natural cover is available
- Curling up to look like a boulder
- Standing motionless in a field to pass for a scarecrow
- Hiding in sacks, baskets, barrels, and other containers
- Hiding under floors or in crawl spaces

## Basic Rules of Camouflage

### What Camouflage Cannot Do

Camouflage hides the body; it does not protect it from weapons and grenades. Even total darkness does not automatically mean safety: the opponent may use night-vision devices, infrared, or thermal imaging. If a searchlight or a flare catches you, make yourself small and stop moving.

### Clothing and Camouflage Materials

In the dark, colors disappear. Whether you wear bright red or black makes no difference then. But since light can fall on you at any moment, darker colors are the better choice. An irregular pattern and matte fabrics are even better than deep black. Face and hands are also camouflaged in an irregular pattern.

Natural camouflage has to be renewed often, because wilted or dried-out greenery stands out. It must match the region and be attached the way the plants grow: the undersides of the leaves face inward. Artificial camouflage such as strips, camouflage nets, and garlands works much like natural vegetation.

### Movement and Tracks

In half-light, quick, hectic movements give you away. So you move slowly and smoothly. When you have to be fast, stay close to the ground and keep it brief. Movements across the opponent’s line of sight are especially dangerous.

To cross open ground, plan the route in advance and dash from cover to cover, never in a straight line for long. Avoid leaving tracks or remove them, and cover or carry away fresh soil from any digging. Avoid cast shadows from objects or people, and never let a light source be seen under any circumstances.

### The Scabbard as a Probe

In total darkness the ninja found the way with a trick. He set the _saya_ (鞘) on the tip of his blade and held its cord in his teeth so it would not fall off. The scabbard became a probe. With the double length of scabbard and blade, he always stayed out of reach of whatever he touched with it, object or person. If he bumped into an opponent, he simply let the _saya_ drop and had the sword in hand, ready to strike.

### Time and Weather

Ideally, you scout an area in daylight and memorize the terrain. Moonlit nights are best for observation missions, moonless nights for infiltration, exfiltration, and combat. Rain muffles your own sounds and limits the opponent’s view. Guards also grow less alert in the rain and spend less time outdoors.

### Seeing at Night

The eye needs up to 20 minutes to adjust to darkness, but only a few seconds to adjust to light. Never look directly into white light. To read maps or use devices, use red light. When light falls on you, shield your eyes, or your night vision is gone.

Observe from a low position against the horizon, from hollows and holes in the ground. If you stare hard at one point in the dark, you recognize the target less well, and a black spot seems to dance before your eyes. Let your gaze circle the target instead. Cupping your hands like a funnel in front of your eyes improves your vision.

The night deceives. Objects seem to move and to change shape and color. They look larger and distances look longer, while a light that suddenly flares up seems closer than it is.

In a direct confrontation, you can limit or take away the opponent’s sight with smoke bombs, chemical gases, sprays, blinding powder, or intense flashes of light.

### Hearing and Being Heard

In a light wind on open ground, sound carries surprisingly far:

| Distance | Sound |
| --- | --- |
| 70 m | Footsteps in boots on grass |
| 100–150 m | Whispering |
| 130 m | Twigs snapping |
| 150 m | Footsteps in boots on a dirt road |
| 150 m | Several people talking carelessly |
| 300 m | Troops marching |
| 600 m | Posts being driven in |
| 1,000 m | A spade striking a stone |
| 3,000 m | Engines and tracks of vehicles |

Putting your ear to the ground improves your hearing. To locate a sound, turn your head toward it and, if needed, cup your hands behind your ears.

Above all, make no noise yourself. Speak only in a whisper or not at all; hand signals are better.

Mind your body odor, too. Smell is the weakest sense in humans, but guards may have dogs with them. So it is best to approach against the wind.

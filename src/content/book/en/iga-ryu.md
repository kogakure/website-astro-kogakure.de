## The Iga Region

Around the year 680, the province of Ise was split in two, and Iga became a province of its own. Any account of where the ninjutsu of the Iga-Ryū (伊賀流) comes from has to start with this region. Ise had always been one of the most important parts of Japan: it had a long coastline, and the Tōkaidō highway between Edo and Kyōto ran through it. Iga, by contrast, became a rather secluded place. Mountains surrounded it on every side except the north, where it bordered Kōga in the south of Ōmi Province.

These two regions, Iga and Kōga, were where the culture of _ninpō_ (忍法) developed. Both were relatively sheltered, just a little southeast of the capital, Kyōto, and south of the Tōkaidō. They were centrally located and played an important role in the history of Japan.

To these conditions came a catalyst: General Ikai, who according to tradition had fled from China and knew strategy and the art of war. With that, the people of Iga had everything they needed to develop independent _ryūha_ (流派, <q>sect, branch</q>) of their own – to protect their existence and as a way to take an active part in Japan’s political development. Two places in Sada in Iga still recall the link to China: Tojin Iwa (<q>the Chinese rock</q>) and Karadobuchi (<q>the door to China’s deep water</q>).

Close by lay two centers of _shugendō_ (修験道). Southeast of Iga, in the Yoshino Mountains, lived the leaders of the sect; south of Iga, in the Kumano Mountains, lay another of its sacred places. In all likelihood, these _yamabushi_ (山伏, <q>mountain warriors</q>) exchanged their philosophy and methods with the people of Iga.

## Ikai and the First Masters

Under the umbrella of the Iga-Ryū there were many Ryū, each with its own specialties and traditions. Yet all the martial arts of the Iga-Ryū are said to go back to Ikai, who fled from China to a cave on Mount Takeo. Above all, he brought _koshi jutsu_ (骨指術, techniques against muscles and tendons) with him. He taught it to many students, among them Gamon Doshi (<q>moralist</q>). Gamon Doshi and his student Garyū Doshi are regarded as the founders of almost all martial arts in the Iga region.

This theory has a catch, though. According to Master Hatsumi and other sources in the Bujinkan, Ikai fled China sometime between 1040 and 1050. He had fought alongside the peoples of Kitan and Xia against King Jinso (Song dynasty) and lost. According to the history books, however, Kitan and Xia were still victorious at that time. They were only defeated when Emperor Hui Zong allied himself with Aguda of the Ruzhen between 1123 and 1125. So either Hatsumi’s dates need to be reassessed, or a link must be found between Yo Gyokko and the fall of the Tang dynasty – along with who brought _koshi jutsu_ to Japan.

Hatsumi Masaaki also considers a third possibility: Yo Gyokko may have brought _koshi jutsu_ to Japan and Ikai the knowledge of strategy and Hicho Kakuregata. The martial arts of Iga would then have grown out of both systems.

Garyū Doshi introduced the Hakuun-Ryū, a school that Hakuun Doshi had developed and named. Hachiryū Nyudo also deserves mention: he probably passed Garyū’s knowledge on to Tozawa Hakuunsai, the first official grandmaster of the Gyokko-Ryū (玉虎流). The Gyokko-Ryū is usually regarded as a _koshi jutsu_ school, but it is also an official school of _ninpō_.

The Hakuun-Ryū passed to Kagakure Doshi, also known as Kain Doshi or Kumogakure Doshi. He taught the system to Nishina Daisuke. Daisuke founded the Togakure-Ryū (戸隠流), which, together with the Gyokko-Ryū, is one of the oldest traditions in Iga. Both styles shaped the other schools of Iga to a great extent.

Hatsumi Masaaki’s book <cite>Essence of Ninjutsu</cite> mentions another student of Gamon Doshi: Iga Heinabe Yasukiyo. According to tradition, he was the ancestor of the Hattori of Iga and supported Minamoto Yoritomo against the Taira because he had been given a piece of land. He is said to have built a castle that became the origin of the Iga-Ryū. But Iga Heinabe Yasukiyo supposedly lived in the late 11th century, and Minamoto Yoritomo in the late 12th century. For this reason the story is doubted. On the other hand, Yasukiyo’s knowledge seems to have been the basis on which his twelfth-generation descendant, Iga Heinai Saemon no Jo Ienaga, built. Ienaga founded a school that was also called Iga-Ryū and was later renamed Kumogakure-Ryū (雲隠流) _ninpō_.

According to another story, Ise Saburō Yoshimori (伊勢義盛) fought in the Genpei War (1180–1185) alongside Minamoto Yoshitsune, Minamoto Yoritomo’s brother. That fits the dates better. Only later ninja books, however, claim him as a forerunner of the ninja.

Each of the various Iga-Ryū had its own line of _sōke_ (宗家, <q>head of the family, chief</q>). The Iga-Ryū as a whole also had a lineage of important figures known as _jōnin_ (上忍). Among them, it is not unusual to find names that were also _sōke_ in other Iga-Ryū.

## The Grandmasters of the Iga-Ryū

The following list names the _jōnin_ of the Iga-Ryū up to the end of the 16th century. The years are approximate. They do not show when someone was grandmaster, but when they were active.

1. Gamon Doshi (1065–1068) and Garyū Doshi (introduced the Hakuun-Ryū, 1074–1076)
2. Unryū Doshi (1074–1076)
3. Iga Heinabe Yasukiyo (1096)
4. Tozawa Hakuunsai (founder of the Gyokko-Ryū) (1159)
5. Ise Saburō Yoshimori (1159)
6. Togakure Daisuke (founder of the Togakure-Ryū) (1207–1210)
7. Kumogakure Gen an (1249–1255)
8. Tozawanyodo Gen eisai (1334–1335)
9. Hachimon Hyouun (1379–1380)
10. Kuryūzu Hakuun (1394–1427)
11. Tozawa Ryūtaro (1487–1488)
12. Momochi Sandayū I (1532–1554)
13. Iga Heinai Saemon no Jo Ienaga (founder of the Kumogakure-Ryū) (1532–1554)
14. Kamihattori Heitaro Koreyu (Hattori Hanzō’s family) (1532–1554)
15. Nakahattori Heijiro Yasuyori (1532–1554)
16. Shimohattori Heijuro Yasunori (1532–1554)
17. Momochi Sandayū II (1573–1591)

## The Families of the Iga-Ryū

Forty-five families developed and guarded the accumulated knowledge of the Iga-Ryū:

Tozawa, Fujiwara, Minamoto, Kuriyama, Taira, Momochi, Ishitani, Hattori, Toyata, Izumo, Ohkuni, Tsutsumi, Arima, Kimata, Hata, Mizuhari, Hanbe, Shima, Sawada, Togakure, Ise, Sakagami, Narita, Toda, Oda, Ooyama, Mori, Abe, Kataoka, Ueno, Otsuka, Ibuki, Kaneko, Kanbe, Kotani, Hisahara, Sugino, Kazama, Iida, Kimura, Iga, Fukii, Kashiwabara, Shindo, Suzuki.

Some of these names stand out. Take the Tozawa: Tozawa Hakuunsai was also the first _sōke_ of the Gyokko-Ryū, one of the nine schools of the Bujinkan.

The Toda are worth a look too. They were _sōke_ of the Kumogakure-Ryū, and in the 17th century they inherited the Togakure-Ryū from the Natori family. The Natori in turn inherited it back when all members of the Toda family had died. The Toda were also closely linked to the Tozawa.

The Momochi were one of the most famous families, and Momochi Sandayū was without question the most famous ninja leader, alongside Hattori Hanzō. He and three of his descendants were _sōke_ of the Gyokko-Ryū and the Kotō-Ryū (虎倒流) in the 16th century. They had also inherited schools from the Sakagami family. In the 17th century, Toda Seiryū Nobutsuna carried on the Gyokko-Ryū, the Kotō-Ryū, the Kumogakure-Ryū and the Togakure-Ryū. Later, the Gyokushin-Ryū (玉心流) also passed to the Toda family. Other names with a direct connection to the Bujinkan are Ishitani, Izumo and Iga.

## Related Schools

The following Ryū, among others, were part of the Iga-Ryū and the traditions of Iga Province:

Hakuun-Ryū, Togakure-Ryū, Kumogakure-Ryū, Genjitsu-Ryū, Tenton Happo-Ryū, Goton-juppo-Ryū, Kadone-Ryū, Kukishinden-Ryū, Gyokko-Ryū, Kotō-Ryū, Rikyoku-Ryū, Tsuji-ichimu-Ryū, Hattori-Ryū, Taki-Ryū, Yoshimori-Ryū, Uchikawa-Ryū, Gikan-Ryū, Gyokushin-Ryū, Takino-Ryū, Sawa-Ryū, Gen-Ryū, Momochi-Ryū, Ryūmon-Ryū, Iga-Ryū.

The list is not complete, because many Ryū have vanished over the course of history. The Iga-Ryū and the Kumogakure-Ryū were one and the same Ryū: the Iga-Ryū was simply renamed Kumogakure-Ryū. The Gikan-Ryū (義鑑流) and the Gyokushin-Ryū both grew out of the Gyokko-Ryū.

Tracing the history of the Ryū exactly is almost impossible. The martial arts were often handed down as _kuden_ (口伝, <q>oral teaching</q>) and never written down. In addition, the _sōke_ often issued more than one _menkyo kaiden_ (免許皆伝, final mastery of a martial art) to give the Ryū a better chance of survival. Other Ryū had only a single designated successor – if the master died unexpectedly, the Ryū died with him.

## Ten Thousand Rivers Flow Into the Sea

In the summer of 1676, Fujibayashi Yasutake gathered the material. Japan was ruled at the time by the fourth shōgun of the Tokugawa house. Fujibayashi belonged to one of the three most important ninja Ryū of the Iga region. At the end of the Sengoku jidai (戦国時代), his family, together with the Hattori and the Momochi, had the best reputation and the greatest influence.

His <cite>Bansenshūkai</cite> (萬川集海) brings together the knowledge and views of dozens of ninja Ryū from the Iga and Kōga regions in south-central Japan. Respected historians regard the work as highly systematic and logical, both in its choice of subjects and in the way its chapters are organized.

### Jo

The work fills ten hand-bound volumes. The first, <cite>Jo</cite>, contains an introduction, historical examples, a table of contents, and a section of questions and answers. It presents the guiding philosophy of the ninja through the example of successful warfare.

The volume impresses on the ninja that a leader who knows how to direct and motivate their people properly can overwhelm great numbers of enemies. If the followers think differently from their commanders, defeat and losses are not long in coming. A single well-placed spy or enemy agent can bring down an entire army. Hence the ninja’s conviction that one person can defeat thousands of enemies. Again and again, the <cite>Bansenshūkai</cite> stresses that ninjutsu is the most effective method of military strategy.

### Shōshin

<cite>Shōshin</cite>, the second volume, deals with sincerity, motivation, and the moral willpower a ninja needs. Ninja use skills that others would call treachery, lying, theft, or fraud, not to mention extremely harsh violence. That is why they need a clear goal and a view of the whole before they turn to purely technical questions.

The mercenary sees things only from a narrow angle. The true ninja, by contrast, is driven to act by an awareness of personal responsibility. The ninja’s intuitive knowledge of this responsibility, laid down by fate, leads them to play their part. The first step in a ninja’s education is to clear away every mental or spiritual obstacle that would disturb this natural awakening.

### Shōchi

Even the most gifted ninja is helpless without the guidance and instructions of a capable leader. This is the subject of the third volume, <cite>Shōchi</cite>: the effective leadership of a ninja organization and the various ways to a successful mission. It also explains precautions meant to make it impossible for enemy agents to infiltrate the organization. Anyone who wants to understand the art of the ninja properly needs a thorough knowledge of the balance of _in_ (陰) and _yō_ (陽), yin and yang.

### Yōnin

The fourth volume, <cite>Yōnin</cite>, covers the _yō_ side of ninja techniques, their <q>bright</q> side. With the dynamic, positive power of intellect and creative thinking, ninja obtain the information they need without taking part in a spying mission themselves. They have others gather it for them, directly or by roundabout ways, and so obtain all the important data for a successful operation. Knowing the enemy’s strengths and weaknesses, they can meet the enemy correctly, while to the outside world they appear to have nothing to do with the matter and never to have made a move in that direction.

### Innin

The fifth, sixth, and seventh volumes all bear the title <cite>Innin</cite> (陰忍). Their common theme is _in_, the <q>dark</q> side of ninja knowledge. Ruses, deception, confusion tactics, and surprise attacks are only some of the techniques they set out.

Ninja could use methods that a samurai considered dishonorable, contemptible, even cowardly. Accordingly they made frequent use of disguises and night operations. They slipped into the enemy camp by a ruse to abduct or kill commanders, and they bribed important officials to help them reach their goals. Every variant of this unique fighting system is found in these volumes, from single combat to complete plans for surprise attacks by entire combat groups.

### Tenji

<cite>Tenji</cite>, the eighth volume, teaches the ninja to judge the conditions of the environment correctly. This includes techniques for forecasting the weather, tide tables, tables of the phases of the moon, and various methods of orientation and navigation. Part of this knowledge rests on centuries of experience with systems such as _gogyō setsu_ (五行説, <q>theory of the five elements</q>), _onmyōdō_ (陰陽道), or the oracle _eki_ (易). The other part consists of scientific observation and of Indian, Tibetan, or Chinese methods of predicting trends and events.

### Ninki

The description of the equipment, <cite>Ninki</cite> (忍器), begins in volume nine and continues into volume ten. That volume, however, is called not <q>tenth volume</q> but <q>closing volume</q>. Perhaps this is because the ninja saw the number 9 as a means of inspiration and enlightenment.

Under the title _toki_ (登器), the ninth volume lists the climbing tools of ninjutsu: everything that helps the ninja climb fortress walls, trees, cliffs, and ships’ hulls safely and get down again. _Suiki_ (水器) covers the water equipment. It draws largely on practical advice from pirates and shows different ways to cross rivers or to move underwater.

_Kaiki_ deals with tools for breaking into locked buildings, castles, fortifications, or warehouses: equipment for forcing door locks, drilling holes in walls, or lifting doors off their hinges. The fire techniques, _kaki_ (火器), close the <cite>Bansenshūkai</cite>. Here is a whole series of formulas for making and using explosives, smoke bombs, medicines, narcotics, and poisons.

## Ninja Disciplines

These are the disciplines in which a ninja strives for mastery:

- _bōjutsu_ (棒術): staff fighting techniques
- _fukiya_ (吹き矢): blowgun and bolts
- _gotonpō_ (五遁法): escape by means of the natural elements
- _hanbōjutsu_ (半棒術): fighting with the short staff
- _heihō_ (兵法): battle strategy
- _onmyōdō_: Daoist principles
- _jūnantaisō_ (柔軟体操): body control similar to yoga
- _ka jutsu_ (火術): use of fire and explosives
- _kiai_ (気合): harmony with the course of things
- _kuji-in_ (九字印): channeling of energy
- _kuji-kiri_ (九字切り): mastery of unseen forces
- _kusarigama_ (鎖鎌): chain and sickle
- _kusarijutsu_: fighting with short chain weapons
- _kyōmon_: practical education
- _kyoketsu shōge_ (距跋渉毛): rope and blade
- _ninja kenpō_: fighting with the ninja sword
  - _kenjutsu_ (剣術): fencing
  - _iai jutsu_ (居合術): quick-draw techniques
- _ninki_: special ninja equipment and tools
- _ninpō taijutsu_ (忍法体術): unarmed combat
  - _taihen jutsu_ (体変術): body movement and leaping techniques
  - _daken taijutsu_ (打拳体術): thrusting and striking techniques
  - _jū tai jutsu_ (柔体術): chokes and holds
- _seishin teki kyōyō_ (精神的教養): spiritual purity
  - _meisō_: meditation
  - _shinpi_: concepts of mysticism
- _ninpō mikkyō_ (忍法密教): the ninja’s secret knowledge of the universe
- _shurikenjutsu_: throwing blades
- _tantōjutsu_ (短刀術): knife fighting techniques
- _teppō_ (鉄砲): firearms
- _yarijutsu_ (槍術): spear techniques
- _yūgei_: traditional arts

Nothing stops today’s ninjutsu students from also practicing areas missing from this list. The ones who suffer are the students of a martial art that stubbornly limits itself to a fixed set of techniques, say 106 weapons and 42 chokeholds, and takes no interest in practical application. When danger arises, they think in methodical, orderly structures. Mechanical training teaches the mind to compare every new situation with earlier practice and to sort it into a category. Objects or tactics that are not on its list then simply go unnoticed.
